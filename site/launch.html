The money rule, from the first dollar: business money moves through the business account. Client pays you → business account. Never personal Venmo, Zelle or Cash App. You need money → transfer it and call it a draw. Start this habit as a sole proprietor and the LLC inherits it.

Why the LLC waits for December. California's 15-day rule: an entity formed on or after December 17 that conducts no business that year owes no franchise tax for it. File today and you owe $800 for 2026 and $800 for 2027 within four months. File December 17 and you owe one $800, in April 2027. A sole proprietorship is a real business in the meantime — it takes money, signs contracts, holds a bank account. What it lacks is a liability shield, and the exposure here is small: no client premises, no equipment. The cap in your client agreement carries that load until December.

Everything else was checked and doesn't work. An LLC that elects S-corp status still owes the $800 — the first-year exemption belongs to entities incorporated as corporations, not to LLCs that choose corporate taxation. No legislation has reinstated the LLC waiver. There is no low-income or hardship exemption. California's veteran waiver covers selling goods, not services. New Mexico ($50, no annual report) and Wyoming ($100/$60) are genuinely the cheapest states, but working from California means registering here as a foreign LLC and paying the $800 anyway, plus theirs.

Incorporating is the one real alternative: $125 year one with $0 franchise tax. But a corporation owes Form 1120-S or 1120 every year even at zero income — $800–1,200 from a CPA — so over two years it runs ~$2,500 against the LLC's ~$1,030. Cheaper in year one, worse in every year after.
