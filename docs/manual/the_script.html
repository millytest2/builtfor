The Ring Test — ninety seconds

Before you pitch anybody, call their line twice, at two different times, and hang up when it answers or rolls to voicemail. Write down 2/2, 1/2, or 0/2.

About a third of calls to small trades go unanswered. If they missed yours, you now have the only opening line in this business that isn't a pitch. Nobody else does this.

Why it works: every other caller that week opens by talking about themselves. You open with a fact about their business that they can verify in their own head while you're still talking. That's not a script. That's someone who actually looked.

When to call

Trades answer 6:30–8am and 4–6pm local. Midday they're on a job with a saw running. The list is sorted Pacific to Eastern on purpose — work west to east and you get four usable windows in a day instead of two.

How to sound

  • Slow down. Cold calls die on speed, not on words. You are not in a hurry.
  • Statements, not questions, at the ends of sentences. Don't go up at the end.
  • Smile on the opener. They hear it. Genuinely.
  • Use their name twice. Not five times. Five is a telemarketer.
  • After you name the problem, shut up. Let it sit. The silence is doing work.
  • Mean it when you say they've got nothing to lose. That's the whole frame, and it only lands if it's true — which it is.

2. The call, line by line

Four moves. Open, disqualify, observe, dig. Then you're at the close.

Move 1 — Open. Honesty is the pattern interrupt.

Everyone on this list gets called. What they never get is someone admitting it.

If they missed your test calls

"Hey — is this [Name]? Listen, I'll be straight with you, this is kind of a cold call. You can absolutely hang up on me.

Before you do though — I actually tried you Tuesday afternoon and again this morning, and both times it went to voicemail. I'm not giving you a hard time, you were working. But can I tell you why I noticed? Twenty seconds and then you can tell me to get lost."

If they picked up both times

"Hey, is this [Name]? Straight up, this is a cold call, you can hang up on me.

I'll say this though — I called you twice this week and you answered both times, which already puts you ahead of most guys I talk to. Gimme twenty seconds?"

Almost nobody hangs up on "you can hang up on me." Giving them the exit is what keeps them on.

Move 3 — The observation. Then stop talking.

Pick the one that matches what you saw. Say it. Then be quiet until they speak.

Opener A — no website at all

"So I looked you up before I called. You've got [forty] five-star reviews, twenty years in — people clearly like what you do.

And then I went to click through and actually learn more about you, and there was nowhere to go. You're crushing it on Google Maps and you're basically invisible everywhere else. Did you know it looked like that, or...?"

Opener B — a free Wix or GoDaddy page

"So I looked you up first. Forty-three five-star reviews, people love you.

But your website's on one of those free Wix addresses — the one with the word wixsite sitting in the middle of it. So somebody put real effort into building that, and then Wix went and put their own name on your front door. Did you know that's what customers are seeing?"

If the address still ends in the default "/website" or "/mysite": "...and the address actually still ends in the word 'website'. That's the placeholder it ships with. Looks like it got started and then real work got in the way — which, honestly, fair."

Opener C — a duplicate listing that says CLOSED

This is the strongest opener in the book. Use it when you've got it.

"Before I called I looked you up, and I found something you'll want to know about whether or not we ever talk again.

You've got two listings out there, and one of them says CLOSED. So some of the people searching for you right now are being told you went out of business.

... Yeah. I can tell you exactly how to fix that. Takes about ten minutes and I'm not charging you for it."

Give that fix away. Free. It buys the rest of the call outright, and it's the single most honest thing you'll do all week.

3. Two numbers, one breath

Then the close, which is not the sale.

The transition — hand their own story back to them

"Okay so — you've been doing this twenty-two years, you're five stars, and you're getting most of your work off word of mouth.

Honestly that's kind of the perfect setup, because the hard part's already done. You're good at the work and people like you. You're just invisible to anybody who doesn't already know you."

Never argue with word of mouth. Agree with it, then widen it.

The offer

Never say a website price on its own. The second "four hundred for a website" leaves your mouth, you've made a one-time transaction in their head, and every monthly conversation after that is you arguing with your own number.
Say this in one breath. Do not pause in the middle.

"It's four hundred to get you set up — that's the site, your Google profile cleaned up, a tracking number so you can actually hear what it's bringing in, click-to-call so it rings your cell, all of it.

Then it's three forty-nine a month and we keep the whole thing running. First month's included, so nothing else comes out until day thirty.

You can cancel any time, and the site's yours either way. Sound fair?"

The default
$400 + $349/mo

Site, Google profile, tracking number, hosting, backups, one change a month, and a monthly note telling them how many calls it made. First month included.

If they insist
$600 once

Site only. They own it, they're on their own after. Deliberately the worse deal. It exists to make the first one look smart. Offer it honestly; don't push it.

If they ask to just buy it outright

"Yeah, I can do that — six hundred, you own it, you're on your own after. Honestly most guys go the other way since it's cheaper to start and we actually keep it working. But either's fine with me, genuinely."

"Either's fine with me" has to be true when you say it. If it isn't, they hear it.

The close — and this is the whole call

"Here's all I'd actually suggest. Let me just build you a real example first. Takes me a day, costs you nothing, and if you hate it you've lost nothing but the ten minutes.

Then I'll walk you through it. You around Thursday morning, or is Friday afternoon better?"

Three reasons that works: the appointment is to look at their own thing, it's only ten minutes, and you gave two specific times instead of the dead end that is "when works for you?"

Lock it down before you hang up

"Perfect — Thursday at nine. This the best number for you?

And real quick, so the example isn't generic — what kind of work do you actually want more of?"

Text them inside five minutes, while you're still their most recent call:

"Miles here — good talking to you. Thursday 9am, I'll have that example ready for you. 310-606-9788"

When they say yes: text the Stripe setup link while you're still on the phone. Not later, not tonight. Now. A yes you can't collect in sixty seconds is a yes that cools down by Tuesday.

4. Every objection

Sixteen of them. Each one routes back to Thursday or Friday.

"How much?" — asked early, before you've shown anything

"Four hundred to get going, three forty-nine a month after that, and the first month's included. But let me show you the actual thing first — then the number either makes sense or it doesn't. Thursday or Friday?"

Never dodge the price. Dodging makes it sound worse than it is. Answer it flat, then move.

"That monthly's steep."

"What are you paying Thumbtack per lead right now? ... Okay — so this is about four leads' worth a month. Except you own it, and it doesn't get sent to two other guys at the same time."

This is why you asked the Thumbtack question. You're not defending a price, you're comparing it to one they already hate.

"Is there a contract?"

"Nope. Month to month, cancel whenever you want. If I'm not earning it you shouldn't be paying it."

Say it fast and without qualifiers. Any hesitation here reads as fine print.

"Word of mouth is all I need."

"Honestly? Best customers there are. I'm not knocking it.
But here's the thing — even your referrals Google you before they call. They're just checking you're real. Right now there's nothing for them to find. So you're losing the ones who weren't a hundred percent sold yet, and you never even find out it happened."

Agree first, completely and sincerely. Then name the leak they can't see. Never make them defend their referrals.

"I'm booked solid."

"That's a good problem. Then honestly it's less about more jobs and more about better ones. Being easy to find lets you be picky instead of taking whatever comes in. And when you do hit a slow month, it's already sitting there working."

If they push back a second time, they mean it. Thank them and go.

"I got burned by a marketing guy."

"Yeah — what happened? ... [listen, all the way through, this is real information]
That's frustrating, and honestly it's pretty common. That's exactly why I do it the other way round — you see the thing before you pay, the domain's in your name, and you can cancel any month. I'd rather earn it."

This objection is a gift. Let them tell the whole story. The person who got burned and stayed on the phone is buying.

"I already have a website."

"You do — is it actually bringing you calls? ... [if no]
Yeah, that's the difference. A site that just sits there isn't the same as one built to make the phone ring. Two minutes, no charge — let me just show you what I mean."

"My nephew's building me one."

"Ha — that's how half of them start, genuinely. And it usually gets to about eighty percent and then stalls out because he's busy with his own stuff, right? [laugh] No shade to him at all. Difference is mine's done in two weeks and somebody's actually maintaining it. If he gets there first, great — I mean that."

Never insult the nephew. You'll lose. Be warm about him and let the timeline do the work.

"Just send me some info."

"I'll do you one better — I'll just build the actual thing. What's the best email? ... And Thursday or Friday to walk through it?"

"Send me info" is a polite no. Converting it into a real artifact and a real time is the only way past it.

"I'm too busy right now."

"Totally get it — that's actually why I'm asking about Thursday and not right now. Morning or afternoon?"

"Is this AI? Is this a scam?"

"Ha — fair question. No, it's literally me and my buddy Daniel, we're in LA. I'll text you my cell right now, and here's a site I built for a client. You can call me any time and you'll get me."

Then actually text the cell before you hang up. Doing the thing beats saying the thing.

"I need to think about it."

"Course. What's the part you'd be thinking about — the money, or whether it'll actually work?"

It's always one of those two. Get them to say which and you're handling the real objection instead of the polite one.

"I need to talk to my wife / my partner."

"Makes sense. Want me to send the example over so you've got something to actually show her, instead of trying to describe it? I'll check back Thursday."

"Can you do it cheaper?"

"I can't really go under four — that's already pretty lean for what's in it. What I can do is start you on the single-page version and add pages later as you need them. That work?"

Never discount the monthly. Reduce the scope instead. Discounting the monthly tells them the monthly was made up.

"What happens if I stop paying?"

"You keep the site and you keep the domain — it's in your name from day one. I send you the files and we shake hands. The only thing that stops is me maintaining it."

This is your real edge. Most local shops hold the domain hostage. Say this part slowly.

Silence. They just... don't say anything.

"...Too much all at once?"

Then wait. Do not fill it. Whoever talks first loses, and you've got nothing to lose by waiting.

Every answer above ends by pointing at the same place: a ten-minute look at something you already built for them. You are never closing the sale on this call. You're closing the appointment. That's a much easier thing to say out loud, which is exactly why it works.

5. Voicemail, text, and walking in

Most of them won't pick up. That's normal, and it isn't a wasted dial.

Voicemail — then text within five minutes

Voicemail

"Hey [Name], it's Miles, 310-606-9788. I build simple sites for tree guys around [area]. Noticed your site's on one of those free Wix addresses and figured you'd want to know what that looks like to a customer. Not a sales thing — I'll just text you an example. Talk soon."

Text, five minutes later

"Hey [Name], Miles here — just left you a voicemail. Made a quick example of what your site could look like: [link]. Click-to-call so it rings your phone. No pressure at all, just tell me what you think."

The voicemail alone gets ignored. The voicemail plus a text with an actual link is a different thing entirely. Always both.

Email, if that's what they asked for

Subject: quick thing about [Business] on Google

"Hey [Name] — I'm local, me and my buddy build simple sites for tree companies around [area].

Looked you up: forty five-star reviews and your site's on a free Wix address, so when somebody searches "tree service [town]" — or asks ChatGPT for a recommendation — your competitors are getting that call instead of you.

Put together a quick example of what yours could look like, click-to-call built in: [link]. Like it, we make it real. Don't, no worries at all.

— Miles · 310-606-9788"

Walking in — build the mockup first

They get fifteen calls a day and roughly zero visits. A person at the counter with something on their phone is unignorable in a way no call ever is.

"Hey — are you the owner? I'm Miles, I'm local, over in [area].

I pulled you up before I came by. Twenty-four five-star reviews and a website from 2019. I actually already built you an example — mind if I show you on my phone real quick? Two minutes."

Bring the mockup already built. Turning up empty-handed makes it a cold call with travel time.
