1. What we're building

The business in one page.

What we are

We're the outsourced tech guy for local businesses too small to hire one. Not a marketing agency, not "AI automation." Closest honest description: a repair-and-upkeep service for a business's online presence, priced like a job.

Owner.com proved this model works and what it's worth — website plus optimization plus automation, bundled, $249–499/mo — but it only exists for restaurants. Nobody built it for the trades. That's the opening.

The rule that matters most: never quote a website price on its own. The moment you say "$400 for a website" you've made it a one-time sale, and every recurring conversation after that is an upsell fighting your own anchor. Two numbers, one breath, every time.

Why the monthly sticks

  • We host it. If they own the hosting, the monthly is a favor. If we host, it's the utility bill.
  • A tracking number on every site (~$1.50/mo). It proves the site works — "23 calls last month" — and it surfaces missed calls, which sells the AI upgrade as a fact, not a pitch.
  • The monthly report. Without it you're charging for invisible work.

Who we call

Any local business that's phone-dependent, good at their work, and invisible online. Highest-ticket fits are the trades — septic, tree, fencing, plumbing, HVAC — because one extra job pays our fee for a year. Barbershops, cafés and trainers are fine for fast cash and case studies but won't carry $349/mo long-term.

Skip: dentists, med spas, chiropractors, realtors and gyms in big metros. They get fifteen identical calls a day.

3. The script

Direction, not a recital. The energy comes from the find, not the sale.

Before you dial

  • Slow down. Cold calls fail on speed, not words.
  • Statements, not questions. Drop your pitch at the end of sentences.
  • Smile on the opener. They hear it. Use their name twice, max.
  • After you name the problem, shut up. Let the silence do the work.
  • Don't be attached. There are 700 more on the list. That's what makes you sound confident instead of hungry.

Warm opener — your best channel

"Hey — random one. You know I've been building websites and doing the Google stuff for local businesses? I looked up your shop and honestly you're killing it on reviews but there's basically nothing online. Want me to just make you something and show you? No charge to look."

If it's a referral, always lead with the name: "Dave over at [Business] gave me your number — said you might be the guy to talk to."

Cold opener — honesty as pattern interrupt

"Hey, is this [Name]? I'll be straight with you, this is kind of a cold call. You can hang up on me if you want. Gimme twenty seconds and then tell me to get lost?"

"Appreciate it. So it's me and my buddy, we build websites for [trade] guys. I was actually looking you up before I called — you've got a five-star rating and no website at all. So if somebody Googles '[trade] in [town]' right now, they're not finding you. They're finding the other guys. Did you know it was like that, or...?"

Then stop talking. This is where the call becomes a conversation.

Work-first — text or email, no call

"Hey [Name] — I build sites for [trade] companies around [town]. Noticed you didn't have one so I went ahead and made you an example: [link]. No charge, no catch, it's yours to look at. If you like it I'll make it real. If not, no worries at all. — Miles"

Discovery — this is what makes you human

Ask three or four. Actually listen. Write down their words; you'll use them in the close.

  • "How long you been doing this?"
  • "How do people find you right now — mostly word of mouth?"
  • "What kind of work do you actually want more of?" — this becomes the headline
  • "When someone calls and you're up a tree, what happens to that call?" — sets up the AI upsell
  • "Busy season coming up?"

The offer

"It's four hundred to get you set up — that's the site, your Google profile fixed up, click-to-call so it rings your cell, all of it. Then it's three forty-nine a month and we keep the whole thing running. First month's included, so nothing else comes out until day thirty. Sound fair?"

3b. Objections

The ones you'll actually hear, in rough order of frequency.

"I get all my work by word of mouth."

"Honestly that's the best kind of customer you can get — I'm not knocking it. But even your referrals Google you before they call, just to make sure you're real. Right now there's nothing to find. So you're losing the ones who weren't a hundred percent sold — and you never even know it happened."

"My nephew is going to build me one."

"Ha — honestly that's how half of them start. And it usually gets to about eighty percent and stalls out because he's busy with his own stuff, right? No shade to him. The difference is mine's done this week and somebody's actually maintaining it. If he gets to it first, great, genuinely."

"Is there a monthly? I don't want another subscription."

"Totally get it. Yeah — three forty-nine, cancel any time, no contract. Covers hosting, updates, your Google, reviews. If you'd rather just buy the site outright I'll do six hundred, but then when your hours change or you want a photo swapped, that's on you. Most guys don't want that headache."

"I'm booked solid, I don't need more work."

"That's a good problem. Then it's less about more jobs and more about better ones — showing up online lets you be pickier and charge more instead of taking whatever comes in. And when you do have a slow month, it's already there working."

"I tried this before and it didn't work."

"Yeah, what happened? ... That's frustrating, and honestly pretty common. That's why I do it different — you see the thing before you pay, and if it's not making a difference you cancel. No contract. I'd rather earn it every month."

"How much?" (asked early, before value)

"Depends what you need, but way less than you'd think — four to six hundred to get going. Let me show you what I'd actually build first, then the number makes sense or it doesn't. Fair?"

"I already have a website."

"Nice — is it actually bringing you calls? ... Yeah, that's the difference. A site that just sits there isn't the same as one built to make the phone ring. Two minutes, no charge, let me show you what I mean."

"I'm too busy / not interested."

"No worries at all, I'll be quick — want me to just text you what I found so you can look when you've got a sec? Worst case you delete it."

"Send me some info."

"Sure — what's the best email? And real quick so I'm not sending you junk: is it more that you want more jobs, or more that you want to look legit when people check you out?"

"I need to think about it."

"Course. What's the part you'd be thinking about — the money, or whether it'll actually work?" (It's always one of those two. Now handle the real one.)

"Can you do it cheaper?"

"I can't really go under four — that's already lean. What I can do is start you on the single-page version and add pages later as you need them. That keep it workable?"

"How do I know you're legit?"

"Ha, fair question. No — it's literally me and my buddy, we're in LA. I'll text you my cell right now, and here's a site I built for a client. You can call me anytime, you'll get me."

"What's the contract? Am I locked in?"

"No contract. Month to month, cancel whenever. If I'm not earning it you shouldn't be paying it."

4. The close

The moment they say anything like yes, stop selling. Pitching past the yes is the number one mistake.

The words

"Cool — let's just do it. I'll text you a payment link right now for the four hundred, and while I've got you let me grab a few things so I can start today."

If they hesitate on paying now:

"No problem — two hundred to start and two hundred when you see it live. Either way works."

For a real skeptic (use sparingly, but it closes them):

"Tell you what — let me build the real thing this week. You look at it, and if you like it you pay me then. If you don't, I've lost an afternoon, no hard feelings."

Nine things you can't hang up without

  1. Payment sent or scheduled
  2. Business name exactly as they want it shown
  3. Services to lead with, in their words
  4. Hours and service area
  5. Photos — "text me 10–15 pics of your trucks and jobs" (biggest cause of delay)
  6. Domain — do they own one, and do they have the login?
  7. Google access — "I'll send a request to manage your listing, just hit accept"
  8. A specific delivery date
  9. The next call booked

5. Delivery

Five days to live, then fifteen minutes a month. Same process every time — that's what makes it something you can eventually hand to someone else.

The first five days

DayWhat happens
0Receipt and a "here's what happens next" text. Create their client file. Run the BEFORE audit — this is the baseline everything gets measured against.
1–2Build the site from the generated brief. Request access to their Google Business Profile as Manager (never ask for ownership).
3–4Send the live preview link and ask for changes. People need to feel ownership of it — a client who requested two tweaks is far less likely to churn.
5Go live. Send the AFTER audit next to the BEFORE. This side-by-side is what earns the retainer — it's proof, not a claim.
5Ask for the referral while they're happy: "Who else around here needs this?" Highest-yield question in the whole business.

What "done" means (non-negotiable)

  • Google profile section grades A on the AFTER audit
  • 15+ photos live, hours set, description written
  • Site has tap-to-call and a working contact form
  • Tracking number installed and forwarding
  • BEFORE and AFTER both delivered to the client

If the AFTER report isn't green, the job isn't done. The report is the product.

Every month after

  • Uptime check, backups, any content or hours updates
  • Google profile post, photo refresh, respond to any reviews
  • Review requests going out
  • The monthly report — calls, clicks, reviews, what changed
  • One line of commentary: "you got 23 calls, you missed 14 of them" (that sentence sells the next add-on without you pitching anything)
