1. What this actually is

You've asked five times. Here it is, and it holds everything.

You are the front desk for businesses that can't afford one.

A restaurant has a host. A doctor has a receptionist. A law firm has someone at the front. A tree service has nothing — calls go to voicemail, people can't find them, nobody follows up on an estimate, and a customer from three years ago never hears from them again.

That's the job. And it explains every single thing you sell, in order:

That's why it isn't four offerings. It's one job with a clear scope, and the scope has an edge you can defend. A front desk isn't marketing. Nobody asks their receptionist to prove ROI. Nobody cancels the front desk in a slow month. It just has to be there, and that's exactly why the monthly makes sense.

The three words you actually say

"I build websites."

Don't dress it up. Every other person calling that septic owner is saying "AI-powered growth systems" or "digital transformation." More impressive reads as more suspicious. He's been pitched by ten people using words he'd have to look up, and he said no to all of them.

"I build websites" is understood instantly, sounds like a trade instead of a scheme, and costs him nothing to evaluate. The boringness is the moat. Everything else you explain in the next sentence, after he already knows what you are.

Keep "front desk" for your own head and for explaining it to a friend. Say "I build websites" on the phone.

The friction, the market, and the seven things that separate you.

Trades are the densest pocket of no-website businesses in the economy. If you'd picked dentists you'd be fishing in a 20% pond.

And the part that cuts against you: about a third think a website isn't relevant to them. That's the "booked solid, don't need one" answer and it's a real no. Expect to hang up on a third of the people who pick up. That's the disqualifier working, not failure.

But notice why. The top barrier isn't "I considered it and decided against it." It's a belief — and beliefs move in a two-minute conversation. That's literally what your opener does.

Pool math: per 1,000 trades businesses, ~500 have no site, ~170 are hard nos, ~330 are plausible buyers. You need 20 to hit $5K.

They are not getting hundreds of calls

Fifteen calls a day is what a dentist in Dallas gets. Or a med spa in Phoenix, a roofer in Atlanta. Those are the categories every guru course teaches, in the top 25 metros.

A septic guy in Hahira, Georgia with no website is getting one or two of these a month. Nobody's fighting over him, because a business with no website is invisible to the scraped lead lists your competitors buy. That's the design of your list, not luck.

4. The call

Goal: a booked ten minutes. Not a sale.

Before you dial — ninety seconds

  1. Google Maps. Website button? None or a free wixsite address → dial. Their own domain → skip the row.
  2. Ring test. Call the line twice at different times. Note 2/2, 1/2, 0/2.
  3. Know the one broken thing before you speak.

1 · Open — lead with the finding, not with yourself

The opener

"Hey, is this [Name]? — Miles here, you don't know me.

I'll be quick, I know you're working. I looked your business up this morning and there's something on your Google listing you'd probably want to know about. Twenty seconds and then I'll let you go — that alright?"

Why this beats "I'll be straight with you, this is a cold call"

  • It's about him, not about you. The old opener was a frame about your own call. This one leads with a fact about his business.
  • No apologising, no asking permission to pitch. You're asking permission to tell him something, which is a much smaller ask.
  • "I know you're working" — true at 7am, and it signals you understand trades.
  • "Then I'll let you go" gives him the exit without inviting the hang-up. "You can hang up on me" plants the idea.
  • Twenty seconds is a real promise. Keep it. Then he'll give you five minutes.

First three seconds: he will answer gruffly, mid-job, with a saw running. That's normal. Slow down, smile, say his name once.

2 · Disqualify — one question

"Quick one — have you ever had a website, or did you just never get around to it?"

He saysYou do
"Had one. Waste of money."Best lead you'll get. Keep going.
"Been meaning to."Live lead. Inertia, not refusal.
"Don't need one, I'm booked.""Fair enough, that's a good problem to have. Appreciate you picking up." Hang up.

One in three will say that. Don't spend twenty minutes on a no — your only scarce resource is dials.

3 · The observation — then stop talking

No website

"You've got forty five-star reviews and when I went to click through and actually learn more about you, there was nowhere to go. You're crushing it on Maps and you're invisible everywhere else. Did you know that?"

A free Wix page

"Your site's on one of those free Wix addresses — the one with wixsite sitting in the middle. Somebody put real work into that and Wix put their own name on your front door. Did you know that's what customers see?"

A listing that says CLOSED — give this fix away free

"One of your two listings says CLOSED. So people searching for you right now are being told you went out of business. I'll tell you exactly how to fix it, takes ten minutes, I'm not charging you for that."

That last one is the most valuable question here. When he tells you the number, he just justified your price in his own words.

5 · The offer — two numbers, one breath

"It's five hundred to get you set up — the site, your Google listing cleaned up, click-to-call so it rings your cell, all of it. Then ninety-nine a month and I keep it running. First month's included. You can cancel any time and the site's yours either way. Sound fair?"

6 · Close the appointment, not the sale

"Here's all I'd suggest — let me build you a real example first. Takes me a day, costs you nothing, hate it and you've lost nothing. Then I'll walk you through it. You around Thursday morning, or is Friday afternoon better?"

Then: "Best number for you? And what kind of work do you actually want more of?" Text them inside five minutes. A yes means you text the Stripe link before you hang up.

The five objections you'll actually hear

"How much?"

"Five hundred, then ninety-nine a month, first month included. Let me show you the thing first. Thursday or Friday?"

"Word of mouth is all I need." — you'll hear this most

"Honestly? Best customers there are. But even your referrals Google you before they call, just checking you're real — and right now there's nothing to find. You lose the ones who weren't sure yet and you never find out it happened."

"I'm booked solid."

"Good problem. Then it's about better jobs, not more. Being easy to find lets you be picky instead of taking whatever comes in."

"I got burned by a marketing guy."

"Yeah — what happened? [listen, all the way through] That's why I do it backwards: you see it before you pay, the domain's in your name, cancel any month. I'd rather earn it."

"Just send me some info."

"I'll do you one better — I'll build the actual thing. Best email? And Thursday or Friday to walk through it?"

  • Pull the tracking-number call count

That one line is the entire reason the $99 keeps clearing.

Day 60 — the diagnosis call

"Now the foundation's handled — where do you actually lose jobs?" Listen for exactly three things: missed calls, unclosed estimates, dormant customers. Sell one, only when their own call log names it. Never invent a custom fourth thing.

One weekend and you can sell on Monday.

The 30-day trial is what makes "first month included" literally true. Turn on automatic receipts, smart retries and dunning (this is how you stop losing MRR to expired cards), the card updater, and Apple/Google Pay. Fees are 2.9% + 30¢, so $500 nets about $485. Start the subscription at launch, not at the sale.

Week 1 — the legal minimum

California charges an $800 minimum franchise tax every year whether you make money or not. Budget for it now.

Week 2, then ongoing

  • Insurance: general liability + E&O, ~$800/yr, bind online in fifteen minutes. Get it before the first stranger pays.
  • Client agreement signed before any build starts. Draft is in legal/.
  • Never run a client payment through a personal account. Commingling destroys the liability protection you just paid for.
  • Set aside 30% of profit in a second account. Nobody is withholding for you now.
  • Quarterly estimated taxes (1040-ES federal, 540-ES California) once profitable.
  • One hour with a CPA before year end. About $300. Worth every dollar.
  • Client domains registered in the client's name. You're a Manager on their Google profile, never the Owner.

When you hire your friend — client #6

Not before (you need to have done the work to teach it), not after (you become the bottleneck). Contractor agreement signed and a W-9 in hand before the first payment. Without the IP assignment clause in that agreement, your friend owns the site you already sold to a client. 1099-NEC in January if you paid him $600+.
