2. Why 7 days was wrong

You caught this and you're right. Here's the specific reason, and the fix.

The build isn't the bottleneck — the client is

Roughly 12–15 hours of actual work fits inside a week. But every single dependency sits on their side: intake answers, photos, testimonials, logo files, domain login, Google access, approval on the message, one revision round. Photos alone routinely take a week. A 7-day promise means you are late on nearly every project for reasons that aren't yours — and you eat the blame anyway.

Three more reasons it breaks:

  • Message work needs a beat. Positioning isn't typing — it needs a draft, a night of thought, and a round of back-and-forth. Compressing that produces generic copy, which is the one thing this tier is supposed to be better at.
  • Four builds a month means they overlap. Two people part-time cannot run four independent 7-day sprints while also selling. Week two of one build is week one of the next.
  • Real-world friction: DNS propagation, booking tools that need their logins, a payment processor if they take deposits.
The fix: 14 days, two phases, preview by day 5. You still look extraordinarily fast — agencies quote six to twelve weeks — and you stop setting yourself up to be late. Under-promise by a week and you get to over-deliver instead of apologize.

The two phases

PhaseDaysWhat lands
1 — Live & bookable1–7Message approved, core site built and live, one booking flow working. They can start using it.
2 — Converting8–14Proof and testimonials placed, follow-up sequence wired, tracking installed, search basics, walkthrough call.

Phase 1 gives them a visible win in week one, which keeps them engaged and makes the second week easy. Phase 2 is the part that actually makes it convert.

Not included — say this out loud on the call

  • No SEO retainer, no paid ads, no content calendar, no social posting
  • No logo or brand identity design
  • One booking flow, not three. One follow-up sequence, not a funnel.
  • Two revision rounds, then it ships
  • No custom integrations beyond the one booking tool

3. The career sector — the UPath play

Why this is the right premium niche for you specifically, not just a higher-priced one.

The case

  • The money is real. Private college admissions consulting runs $5,000–25,000 per family. Career coaching packages run $1,500–7,500. At those numbers a $2,000 build is a rounding error, not a decision.
  • It's the market UPath lives in. Every call teaches you how education and career buyers think, what they pay for, and what they object to. That's paid market research for the company you actually care about.
  • The relationships compound. Admissions consultants and career coaches sit on top of exactly the audience UPath needs. They are future partners, design partners, and distribution.
  • Client #1 was a trainer. Your warm network already skews coaching and people-services. This is not a cold start.

The 30-second qualification test (List B)

You can't automate this one — but it takes half a minute per name:

  1. Open their site on your phone. Does it load fast and read well?
  2. Can you tell in 10 seconds who it's for and what it costs?
  3. Is there a real booking link — a calendar — or just a contact form?
  4. Is their proof visible — results, testimonials, credentials — or buried?
  5. Is the owner the decision-maker? Solo or 2–3 people, not a 20-person firm.

Two or more failures and they're a live target. If it passes all five, move on — they don't need you and you'll waste the call.

The pitch, adapted

"You're clearly good at this — I can see the results you're getting for students. But when a parent finds you, there's no clear way to understand your process or actually book a consult. They just get a contact form and silence. I can fix that in two weeks."

Same structure that worked for septic companies: name real proof, then name the gap. The only thing that changed is that the gap is a booking path instead of a missing website.
