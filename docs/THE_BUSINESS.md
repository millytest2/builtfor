## 1. The sentence

You've asked three times, so here it is in the three forms you need.

**What you say to a customer, on the phone:**
> "I make sure that when somebody goes looking for you, they find you — and that they
> can actually reach you."

**The category, for your own head:**
> **A local visibility utility.** Not an agency. Not marketing. Not AI. For a tree guy,
> the internet is infrastructure — the same shelf as his phone line and the sign on his
> shop. You install it and you keep it running.

**The business model, for a friend or your CPA:**
> "I find the places a local business is quietly losing customers, and I fix them. The
> first one is always the same: nobody can find them. Later there are others."

That third sentence is the one that holds everything together — the website, the Google
profile, and eventually the missed calls and the dead leads. **They're all the same
problem.** A business that already has demand is leaking it. You plug leaks, in order of
how obvious they are.

That's why the sequence isn't random and why you never have to invent an offering
again:

| Leak | When you sell it |
|---|---|
| **Nobody can find you** | Day one. The whole business for now. |
| Your phone rings and nobody picks up | After 10 clients, when their own call log proves it |
| You send estimates and nobody follows up | After 10 clients |
| You have 2,000 old customers and never contact them | After 10 clients |

### The three words

> **"I build websites."**

That's it. Don't dress it up.

Everyone else calling that septic owner is saying "AI-powered growth systems" or
"digital transformation" or "local marketing solutions." **More impressive means more
suspicious.** A 55-year-old guy with a pump truck has been pitched by ten people using
words he'd have to look up, and he's said no to all of them.

"I build websites" is understood instantly, sounds like a trade instead of a scheme, and
costs him nothing to evaluate. **The boringness is the moat.** Everything else — the
Google profile, the hosting, the tracking number, the monthly — you explain in the next
sentence, after he already knows what you are.

Keep "local visibility utility" for your own head. Never say it to a customer.

## 1c. Why you beat the other callers

### First, the correction: they are not getting hundreds of calls

You assumed these owners are buried in pitches. **They're not, and that's the whole
reason the target list looks the way it does.**

Fifteen-calls-a-day is what a **dentist in Dallas** gets. Or a med spa in Phoenix, or a
roofer in Atlanta. Those are the categories every guru course teaches, in the top 25
metros, and they're brutal.

**A septic guy in Hahira, Georgia with no website is getting one or two of these calls a
month.** Possibly fewer. Nobody is fighting over him, because he is genuinely hard to
find — a business with no website is invisible to the scraped lead lists your competitors
buy. That's not an accident in your list. It's the design.

### Then, the seven things that actually separate you

| # | What you do | What every other caller does |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Show up with the work already done.** "I already built you an example." | Asks for thirty minutes of his time |
| **2** | **Have a specific fact about his business.** "I called twice, both went to voicemail." | Reads a script with his name merged into it |
| **3** | **Say the price on the first call.** | Dodges to a "discovery call" |
| **4** | **Small number.** $500 sounds like a job. | $3–5K plus a $500/mo retainer, which sounds like a program |
| **5** | **He owns the domain and can cancel any month.** | Holds the domain, twelve-month contract |
| **6** | **"It's literally me, I'm in LA."** | A company name he's never heard of |
| **7** | **Promise a working front door, not leads.** | Promises growth, which he's heard before and didn't get |

**Number 1 is the one that matters most.** Everything else is a tiebreaker. Almost nobody
does free work before asking for anything, because it doesn't scale for an agency with
staff — but it costs you thirty minutes with the generator. That's a structural advantage
they can't copy without breaking their own economics.

**And give something away on the call.** The CLOSED-listing fix, a duplicate listing, a
wrong category on their Google profile. Tell them how to fix it and don't charge. You'll
know within ten seconds whether you're talking to a buyer, and either way you did
something useful.

## 2. Is there actually a market? The honest answer

You asked whether anyone really wants this, and you flagged that your read comes from
social media. Good instinct — most of what you see there is people selling courses about
the business, not people running it. So here's real survey data.

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| **27–29% of US small businesses have no website** (2026) | Multiple 2026 surveys, converging |
| **Trades and contractors: 45–56% have no website** — the highest of any category | Industry breakdowns |
| Professional services: only 15–25% | Same |
| The number falls **1–2 points a year** (36% in 2020 → 27% in 2026) | Trend data |
| **Top reason they don't have one: "not relevant to my industry" (34%)** | Clutch |
| Cost is only cited by **16–23%** | Clutch |
| **21% use social media instead** of a website | Clutch |

**What this actually tells you, including the part you won't like:**

1. **Your targeting was right by accident.** Trades are the single densest pocket of
   no-website businesses in the economy — roughly half of them. If you'd picked
   dentists or lawyers you'd be fishing in a 20% pond.

2. **Your skepticism is one-third correct.** You said "if they wanted a website they'd
   have built one by now." For about a third of them — the 34% who think it's not
   relevant — you're right. That's the "I'm booked solid, don't need one" answer, and
   it's a real no. **Expect to hang up on a third of the people who pick up.** That's
   not failure, that's the disqualifier working.

3. **But the reason matters enormously.** The #1 barrier isn't "I considered it and
   decided against it." It's a *belief* — "this doesn't apply to a business like mine."
   Beliefs move in a two-minute conversation. That is literally what your opener does:
   *"You're crushing it on Google Maps and you're invisible everywhere else. Did you
   know it looked like that?"*

4. **The window is closing slowly, not fast.** A point or two a year. You have years,
   not months. But it is closing, and that's an argument for moving now and for not
   treating this as a forever business — which you already weren't.

**Rough pool math:** take a thousand trades businesses. ~500 have no website. ~170 of
those are a hard no. **That leaves ~330 per thousand who are plausible buyers.** You
need 20 to hit $5K.

**What I can't tell you:** whether *you* can sell it. Nobody can, including you, at six
lifetime dials. **100 dials tells you.** At 70/week that's ten business days. That is
the entire remaining unknown in this business, and it's cheap to resolve.

## 3. The offer — settled, stop moving it

You've floated $500–800, $90/mo, $99/mo, $149/mo. Pick one and stop. Here it is:

### **$500 to build it. $99/month to keep it running. First month included.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Build — $500, one time** | One-page site, mobile-first, click-to-call, contact form, their photos and reviews. **Plus their Google Business Profile created or cleaned up.** Live in 14 days. |
| **Keep it running — $99/mo** | Hosting, SSL, backups, uptime, form delivery, a tracking number, one small change a month, and a monthly line telling them how many calls the site produced. |
| **If they insist on owning it outright** | **$800 once.** No support after. Deliberately the worse deal. |

**Why $500 and $99 and not more:** you're learning. A lower price means more yeses,
which means more reps, which is the actual point of this year. $99 sits under the
psychological hundred-dollar line and never triggers a negotiation.

**The raise trigger — write this down now so you actually do it:**
> **At client #10, new clients pay $750 + $149/mo.** Your first ten are grandfathered at
> $500 + $99 forever. By then you'll have call logs proving the site works, and evidence
> is what buys a price increase.

### The two rules you cannot break

**1. Never say the build price alone.** Two numbers, one breath, every single time.
"Five hundred to get set up, then ninety-nine a month." The moment "five hundred for a
website" lands on its own you've made a one-time transaction in their head, and every
monthly conversation after that is you arguing with your own number. That is exactly how
client #1 ended at $400 and $0 recurring.

**2. Never discount the monthly.** Cut scope instead — "I can start you on the one-page
version." Discounting the monthly tells them you made it up.

### On your "build it free first" idea

You floated building the whole site free, showing it, then charging $500 if they like it.
**Half right, and the half that's wrong will eat your year.**

- **Right:** showing up with the work already done is your single biggest edge. Nobody
  else does it. It converts the call from a pitch into a gift.
- **Wrong:** building *finished sites* free doesn't scale. Ten free builds is thirty
  hours for maybe two sales. You'd be working full time for $1,000.

**Do this instead: the mockup is free, the site is $500.** The generator in this repo
produces a real, sendable mockup in about thirty minutes. That's what you build free.
It does all the persuasive work of a finished site.

> "Let me build you a real example first. Takes me a day, costs you nothing. If you hate
> it you've lost nothing."

Then $500 turns the example into the real thing, on their domain, with the Google profile
fixed.

### The monthly stays tiny — this is the whole point

You said you don't want to do much monthly work, and that Google profile work is
specifically what you just quit. So:

- **The Google profile is a ONE-TIME job inside the $500.** You fix it at launch and
  you're done. It is not a monthly obligation.
- **The $99 buys infrastructure, not labour.** Hosting, uptime, backups, one small
  change, a call count. **Under 20 minutes per client per month, all clients batched
  into one day** — first Monday, every month.

If a care plan ever takes more than 20 minutes, the promise is wrong. Fix the promise,
not the hours. An agency sells recurring labour. A utility sells recurring service. That
distinction is the only thing standing between this and your old job.

4. Phone- and estimate-dependent

**Best verticals, in order:** tree service, septic, fencing and gates, concrete and

**To refill it:** restore the Google Places API key. Ten minutes in Google Cloud Console.
Web search structurally *cannot* find businesses without websites — there's nothing to

the Places API *is* the Google Business Profile website field, so that key is the only
instrument that answers this at scale. **After your first ten dials, this is your
highest-leverage task.**

## 5. What to say

**`outreach/CALL_CARD.txt`** — one page, the whole call. This is the one you keep open
next to the call list. `outreach/SCRIPT_FULL.txt` has all sixteen objections and the
voicemail/text/email/walk-in versions for when you want them.

The whole call in six moves:

1. **Open** — "I'll be straight with you, this is kind of a cold call. You can hang up.
   Gimme twenty seconds?"
2. **Disqualify** — "Have you ever had a website, or just never got around to it?"
   Booked-solid means hang up politely. Don't spend twenty minutes on a no.
3. **Observe, then shut up** — "Forty five-star reviews, and I went to click through and
   there was nowhere to go."
4. **One question** — "When everybody's on a job, what happens to the phone?" Write the
   answer down verbatim. That's next year's sale.
5. **The offer** — two numbers, one breath.
6. **Close the appointment, not the sale** — "Let me build you a real example. Thursday
   morning, or is Friday better?"

Sixteen objections with answers are in the script. The one the data says you'll hear
most is *"I don't need one"* — that's the 34%. Answer once, and if they say it twice,
thank them and go.

## 9. The back end — and when you're allowed to touch it

You keep circling AI integration and reactivating dead leads. Both are real. **Both are
forbidden until you have 10 paying clients and 60 days of call data on them.**

Here's the actual reason, and it's not discipline for its own sake: **the moment you
promise leads or revenue, you own their P&L.** When a seasonal slow month hits — and it
will, for every trade on that list — it's your fault. A utility never has that problem.
A lead-gen guy always does.

When you're through the gate, there are exactly three products, in this order, forever:

1. **Missed-call text-back** — sells itself the month their own tracking log says they
   missed 14 calls
2. **Estimate follow-up** — fence and gate guys send 20 quotes and chase none
3. **Dormant customer reactivation** — septic tanks are due every 3–5 years and they
   already own the list

$200–400/mo each, sold one at a time, **only when their own data names the problem.**
Never invent a custom fourth thing for one client.

**On "AI agency":** never say the word AI to a customer. Say it to a septic owner in
Georgia and you become the fifteenth caller that week. **AI is your cost structure, not
your product** — it's why a build takes you two hours instead of twenty. That's a margin
advantage. Keep it to yourself.

**When to hire your friend:** client #6. Not before — you need to have done the work
yourself enough to teach it. Not after — you become the bottleneck. One build, your
checklist, your review. That's the training.
