# Why you're getting "not interested," and what to sell instead

Written after the first three real dials, 21 Sep 2026.

---

## Your Social Hog observation is the most important thing you've said

You noticed it yourself:

> "At Social Hog, if they had no Google presence and you typed it in and they weren't
> being found — very easy sell. A website is more like a nice thing to have."

**That's exactly right, and it explains all three nos.** Here's the mechanism.

| | The Social Hog pitch | The website pitch |
|---|---|---|
| **Can you prove it in 10 seconds?** | **Yes.** "Search it right now. You're not there." | No. They know they don't have one. |
| **Is the loss happening today?** | **Yes**, every search, all day | Hypothetical |
| **Is a specific competitor winning?** | **Yes**, you can name who shows up instead | No |
| **Does the fix obviously fix it?** | **Yes.** Claim the listing → you appear | Unclear. They doubt a website rings the phone. |
| **Who decided the status quo?** | Nobody. It's an accident. | **They did.** They've lived without one for 20 years. |

That last row is the killer. **A website isn't an unsolved problem, it's a settled
decision.** When you call about it, you're asking them to reverse a judgment they
already made. When you call about a broken listing, you're telling them something they
didn't know.

> **A repair sells. An improvement doesn't.**

"Not interested, thank you" is the correct response to an improvement pitch. It isn't a
rejection of you or of the price. They never got far enough to consider either.

## The test every offer has to pass

> **Can I prove this is costing them money, right now, in under 30 seconds, without
> asking them to believe me?**

Run your options through it:

| Thing you could sell | Provable on the call? | Losing money now? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| You don't have a website | No — they know | No | **Improvement. Dead.** |
| You can't be found on Maps | **Yes** — "search it" | Yes | **Repair** |
| Your listing says CLOSED | **Yes** — devastating | Yes | **Repair** |
| You have two listings splitting your reviews | **Yes** | Yes | **Repair** |
| Your hours are wrong | **Yes** | Yes | **Repair** |
| **I called twice and nobody picked up** | **Yes — you just did it** | **Yes** | **Repair** |
| You're paying Thumbtack $80 a lead | They tell you the number | Yes | **Repair** |
| You send estimates and never follow up | They admit it | Yes | **Repair** |

**Look at what survives.** It's Google listing problems and missed calls. Almost nothing
else passes.

### The uncomfortable part

**The thing you know sells is the thing you said you don't want to do.** You spent a
year on Google profile work at Social Hog, you're bored of it, and you'd rather build
websites. That's a real conflict and it's worth naming out loud rather than designing
around.

**But look closer at what you actually hated.** It wasn't the diagnosis. It was the
grind — posting every Tuesday, answering reviews, the endless monthly upkeep. Two
different jobs:

| What sells | What you hated |
|---|---|
| Finding the broken thing (2 minutes) | Posting to their profile every week |
| Fixing it once (20 minutes) | Responding to reviews forever |
| Showing them before/after | Monthly "content" for a profile |

**Keep the first column. Never sell the second.** Diagnose and repair once, inside the
build fee. The monthly is hosting and uptime, which is near-zero-touch. That's the whole
design already in `THE_BUSINESS.md` — this just explains why the front half is what
opens the door.

## What the business actually is

You keep asking, so here it is in the form that survives this analysis.

**Between "somebody wants to hire you" and "you get paid," there is a chain. You check
every link, find the broken one, and fix it.**

| # | Link in the chain | How it breaks | What you sell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Can they find you?** | No listing, wrong category, buried | GBP fix |
| 2 | **Does the listing say the right thing?** | Says CLOSED. Duplicates. Wrong hours. | GBP fix |
| 3 | **Is there anywhere to go when they click?** | No site, or a dead free one | **The website** |
| 4 | **Does the phone get answered?** | Crew's on a job, goes to voicemail | Missed-call capture |
| 5 | **Does the estimate get chased?** | Sent 20, followed up on zero | Quote follow-up |
| 6 | **Do old customers come back?** | 2,000 names, never contacted | Reactivation |

**You have been selling link #3 as if it were the whole chain.** It's the least provable
link in it and the only one they've already decided about.

Same "front desk" idea, sharper: a front desk doesn't just sit there looking nice. It
makes sure nobody who wanted to reach you failed to.

## The change: lead with the phone, attach the website

**Stop opening with the website. Open with the call you just made to them.**

The Ring Test has been sitting in this playbook as a warm-up. **It's the product.** It is
the only thing on your list that is:

- **Proven, not claimed.** You did it. It happened. There's nothing to argue with.
- **Happening today.** Not a projection.
- **Trivially valuable to them.** One missed tree job is $800–2,500. One septic pump is
  $400. They do that math instantly and without your help.
- **Not a promise about the future.** You are not claiming you'll get them leads. You're
  pointing at demand they *already earned* and dropped.

That last distinction matters more than it looks. "I'll get you more customers" makes
you responsible for their revenue. **"The customers you already have are calling and
nobody's picking up"** is a plumbing problem. Plumbing problems get fixed.

### The three findings, delivered as a list

Before you dial: call them twice at different times, look at their Maps listing, and
click whatever website they have. Now you have three facts, not a pitch.

> 1. "I called twice. Both went to voicemail."
> 2. "Your listing has [two entries / wrong hours / says closed]."
> 3. "And when I clicked through there was nowhere to go."

**Three provable problems beats one unprovable improvement.** Then the website is the
answer to #3, which he asked about, instead of the thing you led with.

## The new opener

> "Hey, is this [Name]? — Miles here, you don't know me, and I'll be out of your hair
> in thirty seconds.
>
> I called you twice yesterday, once in the morning and once around three. Both times it
> went to voicemail. I'm not complaining — I know you were on a job.
>
> But that's actually what I do for a living, so I went and checked a couple of other
> things on your business and I found two more. Want me to just tell you what they are?"

**Why this works where the last one didn't:**

- **It's evidence, not an assertion.** He can't say "not interested" to a fact about his
  own phone.
- **You're not selling anything yet.** You're reporting. The ask is "want me to tell
  you," which costs him nothing to say yes to.
- **The thirty seconds is real** and he can hear that you mean it.
- **"That's what I do for a living"** explains why a stranger checked, in six words,
  without a pitch.
- **Curiosity gap.** "I found two more" is very hard to hang up on.

Then you deliver the three findings, stop talking, and let him react. **His reaction
tells you which link in the chain he actually cares about** — and that's what you sell
him, not what you planned to sell him.

### If the Ring Test came back 2/2 (he answered both times)

> "...I called you twice this week and you picked up both times, which honestly puts you
> ahead of most guys I call. So the phone's not your problem. But I checked two other
> things and those weren't as clean. Want me to tell you?"

## What to do with three "not interested"s

**Don't over-read three dials.** Three is not a sample, it's a Tuesday. You cannot learn
a close rate, a price objection or a market from it.

**But do read the shape of the no.** Instant, polite, no engagement is the signature of a
nice-to-have pitch. They didn't evaluate your price or your credibility; they never got
that far.

### Stop measuring the wrong thing

At three dials, "did they buy" is a useless metric. Measure this instead:

> **Did they stay on the phone past the first twenty seconds?**

That single number tells you whether the opener works, and it moves fast enough to learn
from. Log it in the `outcome` column as `>20s` or `<20s`.

| Result after 20 more dials | What it means | Do |
|---|---|---|
| Under 20% stay past 20 seconds | The opener is wrong | Change the opener again |
| 40%+ stay, but nobody books | The opener works, the offer doesn't | Change the offer |
| People book but don't buy | Both work, the price or the close is off | Change the close |

**Right now you don't know which of those three is broken**, and three dials can't tell
you. Twenty can.

> You're not selling websites. You're calling to tell someone their phone went to
> voicemail twice yesterday and their Google listing says they're closed. The website is
> just part of how you fix it.

Sell the leak. Deliver the fix.
