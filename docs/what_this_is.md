## The category: you're a utility, not an agency

You keep asking what bucket this goes in. Here it is:

> **For a tree guy, the internet isn't marketing. It's infrastructure.**

It's the same shelf as his phone line, his truck wrap, and the sign on his shop.
Nobody calls their phone line "marketing." Nobody shops their phone line every
quarter. Nobody asks their phone line to prove ROI. They pay it monthly and forget
about it, and they'd never cancel it, because without it the business doesn't work.

**That's the bucket. You install and maintain the thing that makes their phone ring.**

Say it to a customer like this:

> "I'm the guy who makes sure that when somebody goes looking for you, they find you
> and they can reach you."

### Why the category matters, practically

| Because you're a utility | Instead of an agency |
|---|---|
| The monthly is an operating expense | A marketing budget line, which is the first thing cut in a slow month |
| You have a defined scope and say no cleanly | You get dragged into "can you also do our Instagram" |
| $149/mo is obviously reasonable | $1,500/mo needs a pitch deck and a quarterly review |
| Nobody expects you to promise leads | Everybody expects you to promise leads, and then you own their revenue |
| You're not competing with anyone | You're competitor #11 saying the same thing |

**And you are never, ever an AI agency.** Say "AI" to a septic owner in Georgia and you
become the fifteenth caller that week. AI is your *cost structure*, not your product:
it's why a build takes you two hours instead of twenty. That's a margin advantage you
keep to yourself.

## It's one product, not three

You're right that it feels like too many offerings. It isn't — you're describing one
product and naming its parts.

**A customer can find a local business in exactly two places. You own both.**

| The door | What it is | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **The map** | Google Business Profile — "HVAC near me" | Create it if missing, fix it if it's a mess |
| **The search** | Their website — where the map sends people | Build it |
| **Keeping it working** | Both, over time | Hosting, uptime, one change a month, a call report |

One product. One price. One sentence. If you catch yourself listing features on a call,
you've lost the thread.

### The correction that matters most to you

You said you don't want to spend real time on Google profile work — it's what you did
at Social Hog and you don't like it. **Then don't put it in the monthly.**

- **Google profile setup and cleanup happens ONCE, at launch, inside the build fee.**
  You fix it, you're done, you move on.
- **The monthly buys infrastructure, not labor:** hosting, SSL, backups, uptime, forms
  that keep delivering, a tracking number, one small change a month, and a one-line
  report of how many calls the site produced.

Budget: **under 20 minutes per client per month, batched into one day.** If a care plan
ever needs more than that, the promise is wrong and you fix the promise, not the hours.

That one decision is the entire difference between this and your old job. An agency
sells you recurring *labor*. A utility sells you recurring *service*. Get that wrong and
in eight months you're posting to Google Business Profiles on a Tuesday, which is
precisely the thing you just quit.

## The offer, final

| | Price | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | **$600 + $149/mo** | One-page site, Google profile fixed, click-to-call, tracking number, form. First month included. |
| **Bigger job** | **$800 + $149/mo** | 3–5 pages, more services, more photos |
| **If they insist on owning it outright** | **$900 once** | No support after. Deliberately the worse deal — it exists to make the first one look smart. |

**Non-negotiables:**

1. **Never say the build price alone.** Two numbers, one breath, every time. "Six
   hundred to get set up, then a hundred forty-nine a month." The moment "six hundred
   for a website" lands on its own, you've created a one-time transaction in their head
   and every monthly conversation after is you arguing with your own number. That is
   exactly how client #1 ended at $400 and $0 recurring.
2. **You host it.** That makes the monthly a utility bill, not a favour.
3. **Their domain, in their name, from day one.** Say it out loud on the call. Most
   local shops hold the domain hostage, so "you own it and you can leave any time" is
   a promise your competitors structurally cannot match.
4. **Tracking number on every site.** It's ~$1.50/mo and it's the only reason the $149
   keeps clearing — once a month you send "your site brought in 11 calls," and that one
   line is the whole retention strategy.
5. **Never discount the monthly.** Cut scope instead. Discounting the monthly tells
   them you made it up.

## The math to $5–10K, full time

| Target | Care clients | MRR | Builds/mo | Build revenue | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$5K** | 15 | $2,235 | 5 | $3,000 | **~$5,200** |
| **$7.5K** | 25 | $3,725 | 6 | $3,800 | **~$7,500** |
| **$10K** | 35 | $5,215 | 8 | $4,800 | **~$10,000** |

Read that carefully, because it says something specific: **at this size the builds pay
your bills and the MRR is what you're actually building.** Fifteen clients is $2,235/mo
that arrives whether or not you pick up the phone that month. Thirty-five is $5,215.
That base is the thing that turns a hustle into a business, and it's what someone would
eventually buy from you.

**Full time, 5 builds a month is comfortable.** Eight a month is when you hand the build
to a contractor and keep the selling.

**The honest timeline:** ~35 closes to reach $10K. At a 25% close on booked calls and
roughly 1 booking per 15 dials, that's about 2,100 dials. At 70/week full time, **that's
six to eight months.** Faster with referrals, which you haven't asked a single client
for yet.

## What NOT to sell yet

You floated leads as a back end. Correct instinct, wrong timing.

**Do not sell anything lead-related until you have 10 paying clients and 60 days of call
data on them.** Here's why that rule exists: the moment you promise leads, you own their
revenue. When a slow month comes — and it will, seasonally, for every trade on your list
— it's your fault. A utility never has that problem.

When you do get there, the order is fixed and there are only three products, ever:

1. **Missed-call text-back** — sells itself when their own tracking log says they missed
   14 calls last month
2. **Estimate follow-up** — for the fence and gate guys who send 20 quotes and chase none
3. **Dormant customer reactivation** — for septic, where the tank is due every 3–5 years
   and they already own the list

Never invent a custom fourth thing for one client. Three products, forever.

## The real reason you're doing this

You said it plainly: you want to know how to put something on your back. Here's what
this business actually teaches you, and when.

| Skill | You learn it at |
|---|---|
| Cold calling, and the psychology of it | Dial 1. It never stops being the curriculum. |
| Handling a real objection from a real person | Dial ~40, when you hear the same one three times |
| Pricing, and saying a number without flinching | The first time you don't discount |
| Delivering under a deadline you promised | Client 1 |
| Getting a business legally real | Week 1 — LLC, EIN, bank, Stripe |
| Taxes, quarterlies, what's deductible | Q1 next year, with a CPA, one hour |
| Systemising work so it's repeatable | Client 3, when you're sick of doing it fresh |
| **Training someone else to do it** | **Client 6** — a friend, one build, your checklist |
| Managing someone's quality when it's your name | Client 8, when their first build is 80% right |
| Building recurring revenue and keeping it | Month 4, at your first churn |

**That's the actual curriculum, and it's worth more than the $10K.** But note the order:
every one of those skills unlocks behind *the sale*. You can't learn delivery without a
client, can't learn training without volume, can't learn taxes without revenue. Which
means the bottleneck is the same as it's been all along.

### The one thing blocking lead supply

Web search structurally cannot find businesses without websites — there's nothing to
index, so they don't appear. That's been re-confirmed three separate times now. The
20 verified leads in `tracker/01_call_queue.csv` are roughly what that method can
produce, and getting to 200 needs the **Google Places API key restored.** `websiteUri`
in that API *is* the Google Business Profile website field, which makes it the only
instrument that answers "do they have a site?" at scale.

**It's ten minutes in Google Cloud Console and it is now your highest-leverage task
after the first ten dials.** Full time, you'll burn through 20 leads in a week.
